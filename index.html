About

I am a researcher in computational neuroscience and machine learning, and a software technologist living in Palo Alto with my wife, three kids, and a dog. Currently serving as CTO at Numenta, I'm passionate about understanding how the brain works and translating those insights into practical machine learning applications.

My journey spans both academic research and entrepreneurial ventures, from co-founding computer vision startups to developing theories in neuroscience. I believe the key to artificial intelligence lies in understanding the elegant algorithms of the neocortex.

Education: I discovered my passion for computers early on, teaching myself programming at age 11. I graduated from Cornell University with a degree in Computer Science, then earned my Masters and PhD in machine learning and computational neuroscience from the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign. I did my PhD research at University of California, Berkeley at the International Computer Science Institute as a visiting research assistant. My Master's thesis advisor was Gerald Tesauro, and my PhD advisor was Steve Omohundro.

Thousand Brains Project (TBP)

Initiator 2024 - Present

While CEO of Numenta, I helped secure financing, create its structure, and spin out this ambitious non-profit initiative from Numenta. The goal of the TBP is to continue Numenta's research into the Thousand Brains Theory of Intelligence. TBP is developing fundamentally new AI architectures based on core neocortical principles like sensorimotor learning, reference frames, and modularity. Although I helped create the Thousand Brains Theory, and am a big believer in its principles, I decided to stay on at Numenta to apply cortical principles to more near term commercialAI applications.

Why Neurons Have Thousands of Synapses, A Theory of Sequence Memory in Neocortex

Jeff Hawkins and Subutai Ahmad
Frontiers in Neural Circuits, 2016

This is our core paper introducing a realistic neuron model with active dendrites, NMDA spikes, and highly sparse representations. We outline learning rules, consistent with recent experimental findings, that expand on the classic Hebbian learning rule. The combination makes neurons far more powerful than previously thought. This theory explains why neurons have thousands of synapses and provides a framework for understanding sequence memory in cortical networks.

How do neurons operate on sparse distributed representations?

Subutai Ahmad and Jeff Hawkins
arXiv preprint, 2016

A mathematical theory of sparsity, neurons and active dendrites that explains how individual neurons can recognize hundreds of unique patterns and how networks of such neurons can perform robust computation.

Unsupervised real-time anomaly detection for streaming data

Subutai Ahmad, Alex Lavin, Scott Purdy, and Zuha Agha
Neurocomputing, 2017

Real-world streaming analytics calls for novel algorithms that run online, and corresponding tools for evaluation. This paper introduces a state-of-the-art, online, unsupervised anomaly detection method that uses our realistic neuron model. We also introduce the Numenta Anomaly Benchmark (NAB), an open-source benchmark specifically designed to evaluate anomaly detection algorithms for real-world streaming use cases.

A Framework for Intelligence and Cortical Function Based on Grid Cells in the Neocortex

Jeff Hawkins, Marcus Lewis, Scott Purdy, Mirko Klukas and Subutai Ahmad
Frontiers in Neural Circuits, 2019

In this paper, we propose a novel theoretical framework for understanding what the neocortex does and how it does it. Based on grid cells, the framework suggests mechanisms for how the cortex represents all objects, object compositionality, object behaviors and even high-level concepts. It leads to the hypothesis that every part of the neocortex learns complete models of objects. Unlike traditional hierarchical ideas where objects are learned only at the top, the paper proposes that there are many models of each object distributed throughout the neocortex. We call this hypothesis the Thousand Brains Theory of Intelligence.
